About the Athens GIS Repository

An open-access web map and geospatial data platform for Athens and the wider region of Greece. Explore administrative boundaries, transportation networks, points of interest, environment, natural hazards, land cover, cadastral parcels, and population statistics together in one interactive map. Every layer is documented, viewable in the browser, and free to download.

  • Open data · CC BY 4.0
  • 81 layers in 8 categories
  • Athens & Attica, with selected nationwide layers
  • Work in progress

Introduction

What it is

The Athens GIS Repository is a lightweight, browser-based GIS for the Athens Metropolitan Area, the Attica region, and Greece. Nothing needs to be installed, and no account is required: open the map, switch on the layers you want, inspect their attributes, and download the underlying data for use in your own GIS software, spreadsheet, or analysis scripts.

The repository is published under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license and archived on Zenodo with a permanent DOI so that it can be cited. It is a work in progress: layers and features are added over time, so it is worth checking back.

The story behind Athens GIS Repository

A great deal of open access data describes Athens and the wider region of Greece. Still, it is spread across many different bodies — ministries, regional and municipal authorities, transport operators, European agencies and community mapping projects — each publishing in its own place, format and level of detail. Anyone who wanted to look at the city as a whole first had to find the right sources, download them one by one, and clean and convert them before drawing a single useful map. The Athens GIS Repository was created to make that first, repetitive step easier for users. These are the main reasons behind it:

  • One place instead of many. A useful selection of that scattered data — boundaries, transport, points of interest, environment, energy, natural hazards, land cover, cadastral parcels, population — brought together so the layers can be viewed side by side on a single map.
  • Data that is ready to use. Every layer is web-ready and described consistently, with its source, coverage area, date, and contributors, so users can quickly understand what they are looking at and download it in an open format (GeoJSON or GeoTIFF for rasters).
  • Open and citable. The data is published under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license and archived on Zenodo with a DOI. Sources are credited, so you can reuse, build on, and properly cite it in research, teaching, and professional work.
  • Nothing to install. It runs in the browser, with no account and no GIS software, so students, researchers, planners, journalists, developers, and curious residents can all explore the city easily — and anyone who wants to go further can download the data and continue in their own tools.
  • Easy to find what you need. A search box and the Athena assistant help users find datasets by what they are looking for, not only by knowing in advance where the data lives.
  • Built to keep growing. It is a work in progress, designed so that new layers and features can be added over time, and shaped by the questions and suggestions of the people who use it.

In short, the repository aims to be a dependable starting point for any study, project, or story about Athens: the data already gathered, described, and free to use.

Repository structure

How it’s built

The site is a static web application: HTML, CSS, and JavaScript with no server-side database. The map is built on Leaflet; vector tiles are read directly in the browser with PMTiles, and GeoTIFF layers are rendered on demand. Spatial calculations such as distance measurement use Turf.js. The web pages are hosted on GitHub, the datasets are served from Cloudflare R2 storage, and the Athena assistant runs as a Cloudflare Worker that only accepts requests from this website and limits how often it can be called.

The project’s structure — the page, its styles and scripts, the dataset files and the layer descriptions — is kept deliberately simple so that new layers can be added by dropping in a data file and a short description.

Using the map

The map opens centered on Athens. On a computer, the layer panel is on the left; on a phone or tablet, it opens from the menu button at the top-left. These are the main things you can do.

Switch layers on and off
Expand a category and subcategory in the layer panel and tick the layers you want. You can show as many as you like at once.
Read a layer’s description
As soon as you switch on a layer, a Layer information box shows its description, data source, coverage area, upload date, contributors, and keywords. With several layers active, the box has one tab for each.
Inspect features
Click a point, line, or polygon to see its attributes in the attributes box. A legend is shown for the active layers.
Adjust opacity and draw order
The toolbar lets you change how transparent each layer is and reorder the active layers, so that, for example, a land-cover layer can sit underneath a street network.
Filter selected layers
Some layers can be filtered by attribute. Currently, you can filter OASA bus routes by line and the wildfire layer by year.
Measure distances
The measure tool lets you click along a path on the map and reports the total length.
Zoom to your layers
One button fits the map to the extent of all the layers you have switched on.
Change the basemap
Choose between a light basemap, a dark basemap, OpenStreetMap, and satellite imagery. The current view scale is shown at the bottom of the screen.
Export a map image
The export button saves the current view as an A4 image, in landscape or portrait orientation, together with the legend and scale — convenient for reports and presentations.
Download data
Each layer has a Download button that produces a ZIP archive containing the dataset and its description file.
Cite the repository
The Cite button generates a ready-to-copy citation in APA, Chicago, MLA, ASA, IEEE, Vancouver, or BibTeX format.

Search and Athena

The box at the bottom of the map has two modes, selected with the switch beside the text field.

Database search

In search mode, type a few letters to find datasets by name, description, or attribute values. Choosing a result switches the layer on, highlights it in the layer panel and zooms the map to it. Because attribute values are searchable too, a search for “bakery” can find the food-shops layer even though its name never mentions bakeries.

Athena, the assistant (beta)

In Athena mode, you can ask for what you want in plain language instead of hunting through the layer panel. Athena can:

  • load datasets — for example “show me hospitals and pharmacies” switches on both layers;
  • close layers — for example “close all layers”, which applies only to layers that are currently open.

Beta feature. Athena is powered by a small open language model running on Cloudflare Workers AI, so it can misunderstand a request or pick the wrong dataset. Check the layers it switches on, and use the layer panel or database search whenever you need precision. Your message and a compact description of the dataset catalogue are sent to the Athena service to produce a reply, so please do not type personal information into it.

Data and documentation

Every layer comes with a short description file. It states what the dataset represents, who produced it (the source), which area it covers, when it was uploaded to the repository, who contributed to preparing it, and a few keywords. The same text appears in the Layer information box on the map and is included in the ZIP archive you get when you download the layer.

Formats

  • Vector layers are provided as GeoJSON or JSON files.
  • Raster layers — population density, building height and tree cover density — are provided as GeoTIFF files.
  • Large layers, such as the street networks, cadastral parcels, and land cover, are also converted to PMTiles vector tiles so they draw quickly in the browser. The download button always delivers the original vector file, not the tiles.

Updates

The data files are stored separately from the web page. New layers are added, and existing ones refreshed, from time to time; the upload date in each layer’s description tells you when it was last published to the repository. Several layers cover a fixed period — for example wildfires 2015–2025, earthquakes 2006–2026 and traffic accidents January 2023–May 2025 — and are not live feeds.

Sources and Citation

Sources and acknowledgments

The repository would not exist without the organizations and communities that publish open data. The datasets in the repository draw on the following sources; each layer’s description names the specific source it comes from.

  • OpenStreetMap contributors — streets and avenues, highways, points of interest, parks, gardens, playgrounds, archaeological sites and parts of the public-transport network.
  • Copernicus Land Monitoring Service — land cover and land use, building height and tree cover density.
  • Hellenic Cadastre — cadastral parcel codes (KAEK).
  • ELSTAT (Hellenic Statistical Authority) and Eurostat — population census data and density.
  • Region of Attica, City of Athens and gov.gr — administrative boundaries and the Athens urban plan.
  • OASA, STASY, Elliniko Metro and Hellenic Train — bus, metro, tram and suburban railway data.
  • Ministry of Environment and Energy — flood-risk zones and rivers; Ministry of Digital Governance — traffic cameras; the Road Traffic Collision Map on gov.gr — traffic accidents.
  • European Environment Agency — Natura 2000 sites; EFFIS — wildfire perimeters; USGS — earthquake records and the satellite imagery behind the vegetation index; SoilGrids — soil groups.
  • Greek Seismic Code (EAK) — seismic hazard zones; Regulatory Authority for Energy (RAE) — wind farms.
  • National School Network — school units; the Electro-mobility Market Infrastructure and Operators Register — EV charging stations.

CARTO, OpenStreetMap and Esri provide basemap tiles. The site is built with open-source software including Leaflet, Turf.js, PMTiles, JSZip, FileSaver.js and Fuse.js. Individual layer descriptions also credit the people who helped prepare the data.

How to cite

If you use data from the repository in academic, research or professional work, please cite it. The repository has a Zenodo DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.18727779.

Athens GIS Repository. (2026). Athens GIS Repository [Dataset]. https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18727779

Or, for BibTeX users:

@dataset{athens_gis_repository_2026,
  title  = {Athens GIS Repository},

  year   = {2026},
  doi    = {10.5281/zenodo.18727779},

The map’s Cite button can produce the same reference in other styles.

Accuracy and limitations

  • Provided “as is”. The datasets come without warranty of any kind. The repository and its maintainer are not liable for errors or omissions in the data, for misinterpretation of spatial information, or for decisions made based on it. Please verify that a dataset suits your purpose.
  • Completeness varies. Layers built from community mapping, such as OpenStreetMap, are only as complete and current as the contributors’ work in each area. Official layers reflect the date their publisher released them.
  • Hazard and planning layers are general. Flood-risk zones, seismic zones, and the urban plan are regional datasets useful for exploration and analysis. They are not site-specific assessments or legal documents.
  • Cadastral parcels are for visualization. For anything with legal consequences, such as property boundaries, consult the issuing authority.
  • Check the date and coverage. Each layer’s description states its coverage area and when it was uploaded.

If you spot an error, or know of a better or newer source for a layer, please get in touch — see the contact details below.
